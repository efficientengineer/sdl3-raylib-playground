# BRIEF — read this first (every agent)

You are one of several writers building the story for a 16-bit style JRPG. The owner is away and
will review everything later. An orchestrator assigns the work and merges it. Do your assigned
part well, keep to your own files, and leave clear notes.

## The owner's direction (governing)

- **Feel, aesthetics, character design, and peoples: Phantasy Star IV.** Manga-panel cutscenes on a
  Sega Genesis. Sci-fantasy: swords and spells beside androids, ancient machines, and failing
  planetary systems. A world shared by humans and several distinct non-human peoples who have their
  own towns, grievances, and dignity. A rotating party that gains and loses members. Hunters who take
  jobs. Warmth and banter between people in a world that is quietly running down. Melancholy, then hope.
- **Story architecture: Final Fantasy VII.** Open in the middle of an operation against a powerful
  institution. A company city that feeds on the land around it. An escape that becomes a pursuit across
  the world. A figure the institution made and lost control of. A past that was covered up (a town
  that was erased and rebuilt). A protagonist whose account of their own past cannot be trusted. The
  institution stays a live threat all the way through, with executives who are distinct people, and
  its attempts to fix the crisis make it worse.
- **Evil is mostly people.** Less "pure evil darkness" than Phantasy Star IV. The harm comes from
  greed, ambition, fear, cover-ups, loyalty to the wrong thing, and ordinary people who need the job.
  Villains have reasons and some are partly right. Whatever is ancient and strange in the world is
  dangerous mainly because of what people do with it.
- **Originality.** Borrow structure and tone only. No names, characters, places, terms, or
  recognisable specific plot beats from either game. No Shinra, Mako, SOLDIER, Sephiroth, Motavia,
  Dark Force, Algo, numans, and no clones of their signature moments.

## Scale (governing)

The finished game should take about as long to play as Phantasy Star IV: roughly a twenty-hour
adventure. Design at that scale: 12-16 chapters, each with a town or hub, one or two dungeons, a
boss, and a change in what the party knows; vehicles or routes that open the map in stages; a hunters'
guild with optional side jobs; a party of about eight that gains and loses members.

A game that long tells most of its story in small moments. Use three kinds of scene:
- **panel** scenes: full manga-panel cutscenes, the expensive kind (one generated art sheet each).
  Spend them on arrivals, reveals, confrontations, deaths, and endings. Roughly 5-8 per chapter.
- **talk** scenes (`type: talk`): conversations in the field, drawn by the game as a dialogue box with
  the speaker's portrait beside it. No new art. Use them freely for banter, town business, planning,
  aftermath, and character work. (Being added to the tool now; format is the same as a narration scene
  with named speakers.)
- **narration** scenes (`type: narration`): text over black, for chapter openings and time passing.

## The art pipeline (why scene files look the way they do)

All art is generated by the owner in ChatGPT from prompts that `./story_prompt.py` builds from scene
files. Read these before writing anything that will be drawn:
- `story/STYLE.md` — locked style, the shot menu, composition rules R1-R11, the review checklist.
- `story/scenes/000_TEMPLATE.md` — the scene file format. `story/scenes/003_the_warning.md` is the
  best worked example (7 panels, 2 pages, staging, acting lines, mood-tagged dialogue).
- `story/characters.md` — the "Design direction" section and the `look` format.
- `CLAUDE.md` — sections "The game" and "Story scenes and image prompts".

Key constraints: a scene is 1-3 pages of 2-4 panels, at most 8 panels; every panel uses a shot id from
the menu; every cast member in a panel gets an acting line saying where their eyes point and what
their face does; a `staging:` line fixes screen direction; dialogue lines carry `[n]` reveal tags and
`{mood}` music tags (wonder, dread, tense, confront, sorrow, hope); no text in images; character
`look` lines describe only what is visible and must not contain Western-fantasy race or class words
or beards (the tool rejects them). Non-human peoples are described by what they look like.
Narration-only scenes (`type: narration`) need no art.

Validate your work: `./story_prompt.py check story/scenes/<file>.md` must pass for every scene you
write, and `./story_prompt.py refsheet <Name>` must succeed for every character you add.

## Rules of the road

- **Stay in your lane.** Only create or edit the files your assignment names. Never edit
  `story_prompt.py`, `story/STYLE.md`, `CLAUDE.md`, anything under `src/`, `story/panels/`,
  `story/refs/`, `story/sheets/`, or scene 003's panels. If the tool or style blocks your work, write
  the problem and your suggested change in `story/notes/<your-agent-name>.md` and work around it.
- **Do not run git commit, push, checkout, or stash.** The orchestrator commits. Do not run
  `./fast_reload.sh` or `./deploy.sh` or touch the phone.
- Do not add scenes to the `## intro` list in `story/playlist.md`; the game plays that list.
- Write for a reader on a phone: plain markdown, short sections, no tables wider than three columns.
- Dialogue: short lines (a 16-bit text box holds about 120 characters), distinct voices, subtext over
  speeches. Let characters be funny. Let villains be reasonable. Cut any line that explains the theme.
- End your run with a report of at most 200 words: what you wrote, where, and anything the
  orchestrator must decide. Put everything else in files.
