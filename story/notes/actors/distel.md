## Voice — five bullets (page vs should-be)

1. **On the page she is blunt; in two places she is *epigrammatic*, which is the opposite.**
   "Both of those are true." and "Not ever." are gavels — lines shaped to be quoted. A blunt person
   stops after the fact. Every composed line she has is in 0180, i.e. exactly where she is least able
   to compose anything.
2. **She answers the question asked — except when she volunteers.** "Klee put them down. My drover —
   I raised him from a pup." is three facts nobody asked for, delivered in an appositive. She should
   say one thing, stop, and let Ottilie ask. Ottilie *does* ask on the next line, so the setup is
   already built; the line just jumps its own cue.
3. **Twice she explains her own people like a guidebook.** "You'd say night herder" is a translation
   gloss for the audience. She has no idea what a human would say. Hand the gloss to Ottilie, who has
   heard of the Mohnen all her life and is thrilled to be right.
4. **Her bluntness is on its way to being a tic.** "She has a name." / "Move your foot." / "you great
   fool" / "Both of those are true." — four corrections of humans in one chapter, one of them in the
   middle of a fight. The two the owner liked are corrections *about an animal*; that is the engine.
   Keep those; take the tic off the boss fight and off the body.
5. **Nothing on the page is Mohn except her ears.** She notices smell, sound and dark nowhere. Every
   sensory line she has is a human's line with her name on it. Three replacements below fix this
   without adding a box.

## Three things only a Mohn would notice or say

All three are **replacements of existing clauses**, not new boxes.

1. **Lamps are rude.** 0160 [3]: "You'll wake her" → **"Take that lamp off her face."** The dark is
   her working condition; a human walking up a night pasture with a light is doing something
   offensive, and she says so before she says hello — because she does not say hello.
2. **Identity is a smell, not a voice.** boss_break: "you great fool" → **"It's me — smell me."**
   This is the one that has to fail. When he keeps coming after she has offered him the proof no
   animal can mistake, the player understands "he stopped knowing me" without a line of explanation,
   and 0180's holes behind the ear land as an answer to something already asked.
3. **She read the fight by smell, not by sight.** 0180 [1]: "Bring the lamp closer" keeps, but the
   half-line before it becomes **"You stank of fear the whole fight."** It is not a thank-you, it is
   not forgiveness, and it is funny in her register — she is telling a boy who just saved her life
   that he smelled bad while doing it. That is a fourteen-year-old, and it protects the humour the
   owner liked without running the tic again.

## Three best

1. **"She has a name."** The whole character in four words: the correction is about an animal, not
   about herself, and it is the first time the player likes her. Locked, and rightly.
2. **"He's looking at me the way he looks at a sheep."** A herder's sentence. No human in the cast
   could have written it, and it is the turn of the fight stated as a plain observation.
3. **"I'm going to cry soon. Keep walking."** Feeling as fact, refused as a stopping-point. Also the
   only line in 0180 where her composure is the character rather than the writer's.

## Three worst

1. **0180 [1] "He's dead. You killed him, and he'd have killed me. Both of those are true."**
   A balance sheet. It explains what the moment means (banned), it is an aphorism (banned), and no
   fourteen-year-old whose animal is still warm delivers a two-column verdict on the boy who did it.
   *Fix:* "He's dead." → a textless **(sorrow)** box → after Falke's apology, "He'd have killed me."
   Same two facts, split by silence, and the second one now lands as a refusal to absolve him.
2. **0180 [4] "So it was done to him. If it was done to mine, it was done to others."**
   A syllogism, and the party's motivation handed over as reasoning by someone about to cry. Her very
   next line — "I'm going up to find out what was done to him" — already carries the intent, so the
   inference is redundant as well as composed. *Fix:* **"Someone did that to him."** Twenty-four
   characters for sixty-eight, and it is anger instead of deduction.
3. **boss_break "Klee — stand! It's me, you great fool."**
   Idiom, which her voice note forbids outright, and affectionate human banter from someone who has
   never talked to a human. It also spends the bluntness-tic in the one place the scene needs her
   frightened. *Fix:* **"Klee. Stand. It's me — smell me."**

Runner-up, only because it is one clause: **"Mohn. You'd say night herder."** She is glossing her own
people for an audience she does not know exists. Give the gloss to Ottilie and let Distel say the one
word.
