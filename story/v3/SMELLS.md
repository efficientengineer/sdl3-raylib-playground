# SMELLS — what four models and one draft all reached for

Five versions of the same two scenes, written blind from the same brief:
**W1–W4** = `variants/writer1–4.md`, **CH** = the two clips in `chapter01.md`
(*The Board* and *The Axe on the Wall*).

The premise: anything two or more of them found independently was not found. It was retrieved.
The list below is the retrieval index.

Note: `chapter01.md` was edited while this was being written. It has already cut its
"It's about me. It was always about me" speech and shortened the Board clip. Where CH is
quoted, it is quoted from the current file; where the cut matters, it is flagged. **The
things the owner deleted on instinct are the same things four strangers wrote on instinct.**

---

## 1. Convergences

### 1a. Plot moves

**C1 — Cray's price is: say Dorn's catchphrase out loud, in front of the room.** *(5 of 5)*
W1 "Say what Dorn calls you and it's yours" · W2 "Say the thing Dorn says about you — out loud,
in front of all these people" · W3 "Tell everybody what Dorn calls you" · W4 "Say the thing Dorn
calls you — out loud, so everybody gets it" · CH "Ask him what he said about you last month."
Given a rival, a crowd, and a catchphrase, there is exactly one cheapest humiliation and every
model finds it. It is the *solution* to the brief, not a scene.

**C2 — The mentor's confession inverts the catchphrase: he told the father he WAS ready, and the
father died.** *(5 of 5)*
W1 "I'm the man who told him he was ready" · W2 "Your father was ready… and ready didn't save him"
· W3 "Neither was he, and he went anyway, and I let him" · W4 "Because I told your father he was
ready" · CH *(pre-revision)* "every boy I ever said 'you're ready' to… I have carried three of them
home."
Models reason backwards from the catchphrase to its most logically sufficient cause. Logical
sufficiency is the enemy of surprise: every reader who has thought about it for ten seconds is
already there.

**C3 — Escalation by repetition: mumble → "Louder." → ALL CAPS.** *(5 of 5)*
W1 "[to the floor] …Pup." / "Louder." / "PUP! HE CALLS ME PUP!" · W2 "…He says I'm not ready." /
"Louder." / "HE SAYS I'M NOT READY!" · W3 "Louder, hunter." / "NOT READY." · W4 "Louder." /
"NOT-READY." · CH "Say it louder, the clerk missed it."
The only volume control a model has is caps lock, and the only way it knows to double a
humiliation is to run it twice.

**C4 — Cray reneges. He took the humiliation and kept the job.** *(4: W1 W2 W3 W4)*
W1 "No. I only wanted the hall." · W2 "[Cray pins the job to his own coat.]" · W3 "Thanks for the
job." · W4 "I never said I'd give it to you. I said say it."
The rug-pull is the model's twist button. Note it also makes Cray *safe*: a villain who was lying
the whole time requires no further thought about him.

**C5 — The axe's placement on the wall is explained as deliberate self-punishment.** *(4: W1 W2 W3 W4)*
W1 "I hung it where I'd have to look at it every morning" · W2 "I just kept it somewhere I could
see it" · W3 "because I couldn't look at the floor it came off of" · W4 "Hung it too high for you
to reach on purpose."
Models over-motivate props. Every object must be issued a stated reason for existing where it does.

**C6 — Lyra is the scene's conscience: one protective imperative, then ignored.** *(4: W1 W2 W3 W4)*
W1 "Give it to him and I'll set your ribs free…" · W2 "Cray. Take the job and go. Don't take this
too." · W3 "Cray, let him have one." · W4 "…don't you dare open your mouth, he's not worth the
breath."
The healer defaults to the moral position. The brief says she wants *to be asked along*; not one
version gives her a want in this scene.

**C7 — Bron's claim to the job is time served.** *(4: W1 W3 W4 CH)*
W1 "I've been standing here since the morning bell" · W3 "standing here since before the lamps went
out" · W4 "holding a spot he has held all night" / "I slept on these boards" · CH "I was there
first."
Entitlement-by-duration is the model's only argument for a character who has no argument.

**C8 — Dorn's confession names its own subject: it was about me, not you.** *(3: W1 W2 CH-pre)*
W1 "It was never about you being small, boy. It was about me…" · W2 "So it was never about me. / It
was always about me. You were just the one paying for it." · CH *(cut in revision)* "It's about me.
It was always about me."
The model cannot leave subtext unlabelled, and the label it reaches for is always the same
sentence. It is also a `not X, it's Y` reversal, which `STYLE.md` already bans — and two writers
did it anyway, because the pull is stronger than the rule.

**C9 — Bron states a want as an ultimatum in his first line of the axe scene.** *(2: W1 W4)*
W1 "I'm going after those wolves anyway. Tomorrow… and you can't stop me." · W4 "I'm quitting. I
came to say it to your face."
Told "state the want out loud," the model states it as a threat, because a threat is the want with
stakes bolted on.

### 1b. Images

**C10 — The pale mark on the wall where the axe hung.** *(4: W1 W3 W4 CH)*
W1 "A pale square on the wall where something has hung a long time." · W4 **"A pale square on the
wall where something has hung a long time."** — *word for word identical to W1* · W3 "A long pale
mark on the wall where something used to hang." · CH "forty empty hooks."
Absence-as-image is the most over-learned visual irony in the language. Two independent writers
produced the same fifteen-word sentence. Nothing else in this document is more damning.

**C11 — The ceremonial two-handed prop transfer.** *(5 of 5)*
W1 "[takes the axe down off the wall and lays it on the table]" · W2 "lying on the table between
them" · W3 "Both hands out." / "[puts the axe in Bron's hands and lets go]" · W4 "[He puts a long
bundle across Bron's knees.]" · CH "[Dorn lifts the axe off the pegs and holds it out, handle
first.]" / "[both hands]".
The handing-down shot. Every model stages it; three of them put the axe on a table between the two
men first, because that is where the camera would be.

**C12 — Night, one light source, in the opening bracket.** *(5 of 5)*
W1 "late" · W2 "That night" · W3 "one candle" · W4 "Night… one lamp" · CH "one chair, one lamp."
Confessions happen by a single flame. There is no version where the hard conversation happens at
noon, in a yard, with someone else in earshot.

**C13 — A small object stands in for the father's body.** *(4: W2 W3 W4 CH-pre)*
W3 "carried what was left of him home in a sack that weighed nothing" · W4 "I carried his boots
home and nothing else" · CH-pre "I have carried three of them home" · W2 "I got there late."
Synecdoche-of-the-dead, plus the "and nothing else / weighed nothing" tag that two of them append
to it unprompted.

**C14 — The crowd is counted in the stage direction, against one prize.** *(5 of 5)*
W1 "A crowd three deep… One slip left worth taking" · W2 "Twenty hunters, one good job left on it"
· W3 "Twenty hunters, one good posting" · W4 "Thirty hunters packed at the guild board" · CH "the
crowd."
Scarcity quantified is the cheapest legible stake. Two of them landed on *twenty* exactly.

**C15 — The paper is unpinned from a nail/board as the power move, then held above the room.** *(5 of 5)*
W1 "[pulls the slip down without turning around]" / "[raises the slip so the room can see it]" ·
W2 "[Cray plucks the pin off the board.]" · W3 "[He signs the board, blows on the ink…]" · W4 "[He
peels the paper off the nail and holds it up so the back row can see.]" · CH "[holding the paper up
over Bron's head]".
Dominance via prop height. It is blocking, not writing, and it arrives before anyone speaks.

### 1c. Jokes

**C16 — The weight of the axe, as the scene's last or second-to-last beat.** *(5 of 5)*
W1 "[lifts it, and sags] Gods, it's heavy." · W2 "It's heavier than I thought." · W4 "[It's heavier
than he thought.] Gods, he swung this all day?" · CH "It's heavier than it looks." · W3 inverts it
— "a sack that weighed nothing" — which is the same motif wearing a hat.
Legacy-is-heavy. The model reaches for it as the closer because it lets the theme become an object
the actor can hold. **Only CH answers it** ("It's exactly as heavy as it looks. You're just
impressed"), and that is the single best line produced by any of the five.

**C17 — The weight line is immediately cashed into a father-callback.** *(3: W1 W2 W4)*
W1 "Everything he carried was." · W2 "He said the same thing. Complained about it for twenty years."
· W4 "No wonder he was in a mood."
Having made the theme liftable, the model cannot resist making it inherited in the very next line.

**C18 — The tough man caps his own confession with a joke or a shutdown.** *(4: W1 W2 W4 CH)*
W2 "That's the whole confession, I'm not doing it twice." · W1 "take the girl and the prick with
you" · W4 "Now take the axe." · CH "Go to bed."
This is the safest possible tonal landing, which is why it is everywhere. The rule "jokes sit next
to the melodrama" was read by all five as "jokes come immediately *after* the melodrama, to end it."

**C19 — "Gods," as the interjection of surprise.** *(2: W1 W4)*
W1 "Gods, it's heavy." · W4 "Gods, he swung this all day?"
The generic-fantasy oath. It is what a model says when it wants an exclamation with no setting
attached to it.

### 1d. Line shapes

**C20 — Cray's first retort begins with the word "Then".** *(4: W1 W2 W3 W4)*
W1 "**Then** you stood a long time to lose." · W2 "**Then** you should have grabbed it at eight." ·
W3 "**Then** he should've signed it." · W4 "**Then** sleep lighter."
The sarcastic conditional that turns the claimant's boast back on him. Four writers, four
different jokes, one identical first word. This is the purest evidence in the file that syntax
converges before content does.

**C21 — "…and it's yours."** *(3: W1 W2 W3 — verbatim)*
The deal-closer, in the same position in the same sentence in three versions.

**C22 — Leading ellipsis as the mark of an emotional line.** *(5 of 5)*
W1 "…Pup." · W2 "…He says I'm not ready." / "…So it was never about me." · W3 "…He calls me 'not
ready.'" · W4 *(implied by the broken line)* · CH "…No."
The only pause a model owns. It appears wherever a performance would have done the work.

**C23 — "Not X. Y." / "not X — Y" reversals, despite the explicit ban in `STYLE.md`.** *(3: W1 W2 W4)*
W2 "Not at them — at me." · W1 "It was never about you being small… It was about me" · W4 "He
calls me — he calls all of us — it's just a thing he says."
The ban is a year old and the reflex is older.

**C24 — The confession is one sentence chained with "and".** *(4: W2 W3 W4 CH-pre)*
W3 "and he went anyway, and I let him, and I carried what was left of him home…" · W4 "and I looked
him in the eye and I said yes, and eight days later…" · W2 "I got there late, and I have been
getting there late every night since" · CH-pre "with his chin up and his guard down, and I have
carried three of them home."
Polysyndeton is the model's grief rhythm. It sounds like feeling and costs nothing.

**C25 — Rhetorical question, answered by the speaker or by the person it indicts.** *(4: W2 W3 W4 CH)*
W3 "That's the plan? / That was the plan, yeah." · W2 "Six? I could have had this at six?" · W4
"Why now? Why tonight…?" · CH "Who signs for you? / Dorn signs for me. / Dorn signs for me."
Fake friction. Nobody learns anything; the line just performs having asked.

**C26 — "Why now?"** *(2: W3 W4 — verbatim)*
The default question a character asks when a prop arrives, i.e. the character noticing the plot.

**C27 — "There it is."** *(2: W3 W4 — verbatim)*
Stock line for a predator watching prey do the thing.

**C28 — The recognition beat: stammer, then a two-clause identification ending "my whole life".** *(3: W1 W3 W4)*
W1 "That's my father's. That's been up there **my whole life**." · W3 "That's — that's Dad's axe.
That's been on your wall **my whole life**." · W4 "That's my father's axe. You've had my father's
axe on your wall **my whole life**."
Three writers, one sentence, including the repetition-stammer that precedes it.

**C29 — "Eleven years." as a standalone opening fragment of the confession.** *(3: W1 W4 CH)*
W1 "Eleven years. I hung it…" · W4 "Eleven years. Hung it too high…" · CH "It's been on my wall
eleven years and I've hated it every day of them."
The number is from `PREMISE.md`, but nothing in the brief says it must be a sentence on its own,
first, as a gavel.

**C30 — The job recited as a three-item appositive: place, duration, money.** *(4: W2 W3 W4 CH)*
W2 "Salt Hollow, cave drake, two hundred gold" · W3 "Cave wolves, three days out, forty mesetas" ·
W4 "Wolf contract, north road, forty coin" · CH "Escort to the coast. Four days, eighty coin."
Quest-log prosody. It is how a model sounds like a game without being one.

**C31 — Exact integers as a substitute for specificity.** *(5 of 5)*
"Eleven years," "eight days," "thirty feet back," "twenty years," "four years," "since you were
six," "four feet behind you," "twenty hunters," "thirty hunters."
Specificity theatre: a suspiciously precise number is the model's way of signalling that a detail
was *observed* rather than generated. It was generated.

**C32 — "It's not personal, I say it to everyone."** *(4: W1 W2 W3 CH)*
W1 "You say that to everyone! You said it to Lyra last spring" · W2 "I say it to everyone. I only
ever meant it about you." / "Everybody says I'm not ready." · CH "You have never once told me I
wasn't ready."
The obvious next move once a catchphrase is established, and all four make it in the same breath.

**C33 — A character repeats another's words back to them.** *(4: W2 W3 W4 CH)*
W2 "He says it to me too." / "Six? I could have had this at six?" · W3 "You called it to a wall" ←
"I called it out loud" · W4 "He's already opening it" ← "don't you dare open your mouth" · CH "Dorn
signs for me." ← "Dorn signs for me."
The cheapest available signal that a character is listening.

**C34 — Dorn's vocative is "boy".** *(3: W1 W2 CH-pre)*
W1 "It was never about you being small, boy" · W2 "I'm sorry, boy" · CH-pre "I was four feet behind
you, boy."

### 1e. Stage-direction habits

**C35 — Dorn's first line is a command to sit or shut the door.** *(5 of 5)*
W1 "Shut the door. You're letting the cold at my knee." · W4 "Shut the door, you're letting the cold
in and my knee keeps a grudge." *(same clause cluster: door + cold + knee)* · W2 "Sit. Don't touch
it yet." · W3 "Sit down before you break something." · CH "Take it."
Gruff mentor = imperative as greeting. There is no version where Dorn says hello, or nothing, or
finishes what he was doing first.

**C36 — The bad knee is spent in Dorn's first or second line.** *(3: W1 W2 W4)*
W1 and W4 in the opening line; W2 "I was thirty feet back with this knee."
The brief hands over one physical detail and every writer cashes it immediately rather than saving it.

**C37 — A beat ends on someone leaving.** *(4: W1 W3 W4 CH)*
W1 "[pockets the slip and walks]" · W4 "[…walks out through the laughing.]" and "[Lyra reaches for
Bron's shoulder. Bron is already gone.]" · W3 "[He signs the board, blows on the ink, and doesn't
look up.]" · CH "[already going] Much worse!"
Exiting is the model's punctuation mark for "scene over." Note W3's "doesn't look up" — a silence
beat, explicitly banned in `STYLE.md`, imported anyway because the exit shape demanded it.

**C38 — Reaching for what isn't there / arriving too late.** *(3: W2 W3 W4)*
W4 "[Lyra reaches for Bron's shoulder. Bron is already gone.]" · W2 "I got there late, and I have
been getting there late every night since." · W3 "Both hands out."
The stock image of helpless love, in both its physical and its confessional form.

**C39 — A gesture used instead of an answer.** *(4: W1 W2 W3 W4)*
W1 "[lifts it, and sags]" · W3 "[He puts the axe in Bron's hands and lets go.]" · W2 "[Bron picks up
the axe.]" · W4 "[He picks it up.]"
`STYLE.md`: "End every clip on a line, never on a gesture." Four out of five put a bracket exactly
where the hard line should have been.

### 1f. Endings

**C40 — The clip ends on a one-syllable send-off imperative.** *(5 of 5)*
W1 "Sharpen it tonight…" · W2 "Go on… Go anyway." · W3 "Go get your job back." · W4 "Now take the
axe." · CH "Go to bed."
Exit velocity. The mentor benediction is the only ending a model knows for this scene.

**C41 — The catchphrase is re-said in the last beat and then defused.** *(3: W2 W4 CH)*
W2 "You're not ready. Nobody is. Go anyway." *(also uses "nobody", banned in `STYLE.md`)* · W4
"You're not ready. [Beat.] I'm going to say that to you every day you live" · CH's chapter closes
"He was right. I'm going anyway."
The callback that reframes. It is the most satisfying available ending and therefore the most
predictable one.

**C42 — Cray, then Dorn, has the last word in his scene.** *(5 of 5)*
The older man closes both cutscenes in every single version. Bron — the protagonist — never gets
to end a scene he is in.

## 2. Singletons worth keeping

The non-defaults. Each of these appears in exactly one version.

1. **CH — "DORN: It's exactly as heavy as it looks. You're just impressed."**
   The only version that hears the cliché arrive and *answers* it. It converts C16 from a theme
   into a character who is not going to put up with this. It is also the only joke in the five that
   is at the expense of the scene's own solemnity.

2. **CH — "BRON: How did he get the notch? / DORN: Badly."**
   A one-word answer. The father's inheritance is a mistake in the blade, not a virtue.

3. **CH — "He swung at a thing he should have run from and it worked and he liked that too much. So
   he did it again."**
   The only version in which the dead man is criticised. Everyone else eulogises him ("the best
   there was," "Best I ever trained"), which makes him furniture. This makes him a person who
   annoyed someone.

4. **CH-pre — "I'm old and I'm proud and I liked you needing it."**
   The one confession with a *petty* motive. Every other version gives Dorn a noble reason
   (grief, guilt, cowardice-in-the-face-of-loss), which flatters him. Vanity is the surprise.

5. **W1 — "LYRA: Give it to him and I'll set your ribs free the next three times you break them."**
   The only version where Lyra fights with her job instead of her conscience. A healer's threat
   is a whole character in one line.

6. **W4 — "BRON: You are the worst kind man I have ever met."**
   Six words holding a contradiction with no explanation attached. `STYLE.md` bans sentences that
   explain what a moment meant; this is what the alternative looks like.

7. **W4 — "DORN: Hung it too high for you to reach on purpose. Then you got tall, so I had to start
   being clever."**
   Admits an ongoing, petty, *comic* eleven-year scheme. Everyone else's wall-placement is a
   monument (C5); this one is a running problem.

8. **W4 — "DORN: I'm going to say that to you every day you live, and you're going to hate me, and
   you're going to come home."**
   The only version that *defends* the catchphrase instead of apologising for it. Dorn stays the
   man the premise describes.

9. **W2 — "BRON: I've never seen that wall without it. It looks naked. Like you shaved."**
   A joke aimed at the mentor's body rather than at the theme, delivered *before* the melodrama
   instead of after it (compare C18).

10. **W3 — "CRAY: Hand it over and I'll put your name in the report. Second line. / BRON: I don't
    want the second line."**
    A specific bureaucratic humiliation with a paper trail. Far crueller than "say it louder,"
    and it tells you what the guild is.

11. **CH — Pip lifting Cray's purse mid-humiliation.**
    The only version in which an outsider with an unrelated want walks through the scene and
    breaks it. Everywhere else the ritual completes exactly as the premise predicted.

12. **W2 — "DORN: That's the whole confession, I'm not doing it twice."**
    A character setting terms on his own vulnerability, rather than the narrator setting them.

### Structural defaults to avoid — not greppable

1. **The mentor's confession that inverts his catchphrase.** He told the father he was ready; the
   father died. Find a reason that does not explain the catchphrase. Better: have the catchphrase
   already explained, badly, years ago, and let this scene be about something else.
2. **The rival's price being public self-humiliation.** If Cray wants something Bron can hand over
   in ten seconds, the scene is a transaction, not a rivalry.
3. **The renege.** The rival taking payment and not delivering. It makes him a liar, which is
   cheaper than making him right.
4. **Mumble → "Louder" → shout.** Any escalation built from repeating the same line at higher volume.
5. **The axe scene ending on the axe's weight.** Any scene ending on the physical property of the
   symbolic object.
6. **Prop placement explained as self-punishment.** Objects do not need motives.
7. **The healer as conscience.** Lyra issuing the moral imperative and being overruled. Give her the
   want the premise gave her, or cut her from the scene.
8. **Polysyndeton grief.** The confession delivered as one sentence chained with "and."
9. **The joke that caps the confession.** A vulnerable line followed immediately by a deflection in
   the same speech. Put the joke *before*, or two lines later, or give it to the other person.
10. **Absence-as-image in the opening bracket.** The mark where the thing was; the empty hooks; the
    wall that looks naked.
11. **The one-word send-off as the last line.** "Go." "Dawn." "Go to bed."
12. **Exact integers doing the work of observation.** Allow one number per clip. Delete the rest.
13. **The uninterrupted ritual.** A scene that executes the shape the premise implies, with no third
    party, no accident, and no one leaving early.
14. **The older man closing every scene.** If the mentor or the rival has the last line twice
    running, give one of them to Bron.

## 4. Rewrite rules

**R1 — When a joke arrives, write the second joke.**
A joke that lands and is then respected is a cliché wearing a smile. The person it hit answers it.
Five writers wrote "it's heavier than it looks"; one wrote "It's exactly as heavy as it looks.
You're just impressed." That second line is the entire difference between the versions.

**R2 — When a scene ends on a callback, end it one line earlier.**
If the last line reframes, echoes, or completes something from earlier in the clip, delete it. The
previous line is the ending, and it is worse-shaped and better. Corollary: if the deleted line was
the only reason the earlier line existed, delete that too.

**R3 — When a character would make a gesture instead of answering, make them answer, and delete the
bracket.**
Every `[he picks it up]`, `[he doesn't look up]`, `[she reaches for his shoulder]` is a line the
writer declined to write. Write it. The rule is already in `STYLE.md` and four out of five broke it,
which means it needs to be a pass, not a principle.

**R4 — Break the ritual: put someone in the middle of the scene who wants something unrelated.**
If the clip is executing the shape the premise predicted, a third party walks through it on their
own business — Pip's hand in the purse, a clerk closing the board, a dog. The scene's obligations
still get discharged, but sideways, and nobody in it gets to finish their speech.

**R5 — Replace the noble motive with the petty true one, and let the dead be criticised.**
Whenever a confession makes the confessor sympathetic, it is the default. Grief, guilt and
cowardice all flatter. Vanity, loneliness, laziness, and "I liked you needing it" do not. Same
pass: if the dead man is described as the best there was, give him a flaw that killed him and a
habit that annoyed someone.

---

## 5. The test — "The Axe", rewritten

Ten lines, avoiding every convergence above. No pale mark, no handing-over, no weight, no "ready,"
no "boy," no ellipsis, no caps, no eleven years, no send-off. Bron has the axe before the clip
starts, so there is no ceremony to perform; the confession is small and embarrassing rather than
large and tragic; and the scene ends on supper.

```
## Clip: The Axe on the Wall

[Dorn's house. Bron is standing in the middle of the room with the axe already in his hands.]

BRON: I took it. I'm telling you I took it so you don't get to enjoy catching me at it.

DORN: Get your thumb round the other side of that handle before you say anything else clever.

BRON: The edge is new. Somebody's had a stone on this inside the week.

DORN: Every Sunday since you got tall enough to reach the pegs. I wasn't going to give you a blunt
one when you finally worked up to it.

BRON: You knew. A year you've known, and you still made me ask every week like it was new.

DORN: Ask me the proper question. The one you've never once asked in this house.

BRON: I don't know what it is. I never do. Give it to me and I'll ask it.

DORN: Why a man with one chair keeps telling a strong lad the roads aren't safe. I like the house
with you in it. That's the whole of it. I've been calling it your safety for two years and you
believed me every time.

BRON: I'm angry with you. I'll be angry the entire road up, and I'll still be back Thursday week.

DORN: Thursday week. I'll do a bird.
```

**What it refuses, and what it does instead:** the axe is already off the wall (kills C10, C11,
C15's cousin, and the whole ceremony); the sharpened edge is a clue Bron finds rather than a
monument Dorn explains (C5); the confession is loneliness, admitted flatly, with no dead father in
it at all (C2, C8, C13, C24); there is no catchphrase in the scene (C1, C41); nobody says the word
heavy (C16); nobody leaves (C37, C40); the last line is about dinner (C18, C42 — and Dorn only
keeps the last word because it is the least benedictory sentence available to him).
