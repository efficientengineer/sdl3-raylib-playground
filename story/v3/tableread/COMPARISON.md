# COMPARISON — writer's clips vs the table read cuts

Six clips, seven reads (three clips needed a second take after failing rule 0 on a fact).
Raw transcripts: `*_take1.md`, `*_take2.md`. Cuts: `*_cut.md`.

---

## 1. The Board

**Writer**

> CLERK: The coast escort is gone. {{RIVAL}} signed for it an hour ago and left at noon.
> {{HERO}}: An hour? I was standing here at the bell! What else is there?
> CLERK: One sheet, and {{MENTOR}} posted it this morning. The bread goes out to the {{STAIR}} on the
> same day every year, and that day is tomorrow, so it leaves at dawn. Two days there, two days back,
> thirty coin.
> {{HERO}}: Thirty? For carrying a loaf? Who has to vouch for me on it?
> CLERK: The rule is waived on this one. No hunter in this valley will vouch for a man on that run,
> because the last hunter who walked it died on that road eleven years ago — and every carter who
> would have taken it instead is walking west for eighty a week.
> {{HERO}}: The last hunter who walked it was my father. I'll take it!

**Actors (cut, from take 2)**

> CLERK: The rule is that you sign before the bell. {{RIVAL}} signed for the coast escort an hour ago
> and left at noon.
> {{HERO}}: An hour? I stood right here at the bell! What's that last sheet on the board?
> CLERK: The bread run out to the {{STAIR}}. {{MENTOR}} carried it in himself this morning — thirty
> coin, two days out, two days back, and it leaves at dawn.
> {{HERO}}: {{MENTOR}} posted it himself? Then where's the carter who takes the bread every year?
> CLERK: There is no carter. Every carter who would take the bread walked west to the lord's city for
> eighty coin a week — and the rule that a hunter must vouch for you is waived on this one, because no
> hunter will vouch for a man on that run. The last hunter who walked it died on that road eleven
> years ago.
> {{HERO}}: The road that killed my father? Write my name on it — I take the bread, I leave at dawn!

**Verdict — actors, narrowly.** "Then where's the carter who takes the bread every year?" turns the
exodus from a clause in a lecture into an answer the player asked for, which is rule 5.
The writer still owns the last line: "The last hunter who walked it was my father. I'll take it!" is
cleaner than the cut's version, and should be pasted over it.

## 2. You're Not Ready

**Writer**

> {{HERO}}: You put the bread run on the board this morning and you never said a word to me!
> {{MENTOR}}: Taking it back down before dark. Forget you saw it.
> {{HERO}}: It goes out tomorrow or it doesn't go at all! Who else is left to carry it — you, on that knee?
> {{MENTOR}}: Your father walked that bread out thirty years. Died on that road eleven years back. I'm
> not putting you on it.
> {{HERO}}: You haven't even told me I'm not ready yet. You're saving it.
> {{MENTOR}}: I'm saving it. Hold the ladder.

**Actors (cut, from take 2)**

> {{HERO}}: You put the bread run on the board this morning and never told me — my name's on it now!
> {{MENTOR}}: Posted it myself. Coming back off that board before dark. You're not walking it.
> {{HEALER}}: It leaves at dawn tomorrow or it does not go at all. Every carter took the western work —
> who is left to walk it?
> {{MENTOR}}: Your father walked that bread thirty years. Died on that road eleven years back. Not
> putting you on it!
> {{HERO}}: He died on that road? Eleven years and you never told me how — and there's no one else to
> walk it!
> {{MENTOR}}: Knee won't walk four days. Been saving "you're not ready" all afternoon — saying it now.
> Off my ladder.

**Verdict — writer.** The actors spent the withheld line instead of holding it; "I'm saving it" is the
whole point of the scene and the cut throws it away in the same breath it names it.
The one thing worth stealing is {{HEALER}} carrying the dawn deadline, which gets her into the scene
doing something other than holding a ladder.

## 3. He Signs

**Writer**

> {{HEALER}}: I will go with him. I am a healer, I have walked that road twice — and do you know he has
> never once asked me to come anywhere?
> {{MENTOR}}: [signing the job over to him] Two days west. Bread on the stone shelf under the step. Straight back.
> {{HERO}}: What's at the step? I've never been out that far.
> {{MENTOR}}: A staircase the ancients built. A woman lives under it and takes the bread once a year.
> Don't touch the stone. Don't go past her shelf.
> {{HERO}}: A woman lives under it? {{HEALER}} — will you come with me?
> {{HEALER}}: Yes!

**Actors (cut, from take 2)**

> {{HEALER}}: Sign it, {{MENTOR}}. The run leaves at dawn — and I am the one who patches whoever goes.
> {{MENTOR}}: [signs without looking up] Two days west. Bread on the stone shelf under the bottom step.
> Then straight back.
> {{HERO}}: Two days west, alone? What is the {{STAIR}}, exactly? Tell me plainly, before I walk it.
> {{MENTOR}}: Staircase the ancients built, woman living under it who takes the bread once a year.
> Don't touch the stone, don't go past her shelf.
> {{HERO}}: A woman takes the bread once a year? {{HEALER}} — come with me. You've walked that road twice!
> {{HEALER}}: Yes — four days, not two: two west, two back. I am a healer, and he has never once asked
> me to come anywhere!

**Verdict — writer, but only just.** The single-word "Yes!" is the best beat in the chapter and the cut
buries it under a correction.
The actors found two real improvements: "I am the one who patches whoever goes" gives her a reason to be
in the room, and "four days, not two" fixes a number the writer's clip leaves the player to add up.

## 4. The Sword

**Writer**

> {{MENTOR}}: Take your father's sword. Six coin.
> {{HERO}}: Six coin? You're selling me my own father's sword!
> {{MENTOR}}: Kept it eleven years. I want paying for the keeping.
> {{HERO}}: There's a notch in the blade. How did he put a notch in it?
> {{MENTOR}}: He swung at a thing he should have run from. It worked, so he did it again, and the
> second time it killed him. Notch stays.
> {{HEALER}}: Pay him, {{HERO}}. He will stand there all night at six coin an hour.

**Actors (cut, from take 1)**

> {{MENTOR}}: [shoves the sword across the table] Your father's. Six coin. Kept it eleven years — I want
> paying for the keeping.
> {{HERO}}: Six coin? For my own father's sword?! Wait — there's a notch in the blade. Where'd that come from?
> {{MENTOR}}: Your father swung at a thing he should have run from. Worked, so he did it again — second
> time killed him. Notch stays.
> {{HERO}}: Killed him the second time? You hung it on your wall eleven years and never said a word —
> here's your six coin.
> {{HEALER}}: Four, and two from the baker. That is all of it — and I am the one who patches you.

**Verdict — writer.** The cut has no joke in it; "He will stand there all night at six coin an hour" is
the clip's one quip and the actors never produced a replacement.
The actors' gain is physical: {{MENTOR}} pushes the sword over before anyone asks for it, which says he
decided this hours ago without a line admitting it.

## 5. The Road West

**Writer**

> {{HERO}}: Where'd you get that coat?
> {{RIVAL}}: Did you honestly think he'd sign for you? The lord of the western city is paying hunters —
> eighty coin a week. I sold the coast escort on to another hunter and signed with him the same morning.
> {{HEALER}}: Paying you to do what, exactly?
> {{RIVAL}}: To walk west with him! He wants rain on his city every year for the rest of his life, and
> he's going to the mountain to ask the ancients for it.
> {{HERO}}: Ask them? People have asked the ancients for a thousand years and they've never answered anyone!
> {{RIVAL}}: He's got someone who can make them answer. You're carrying a loaf of bread and I'm riding
> in a cart. Enjoy the walk!

**Actors (cut, from take 1)**

> {{HERO}}: That coat's new! Where's a hunter get a coat like that in two days?
> {{RIVAL}}: Eighty coin a week, and I signed with the lord the same morning I sold the coast escort off
> to another hunter. Did you think {{MENTOR}} would sign for you?
> {{HEALER}}: Eighty a week for how many weeks, {{RIVAL}}, and doing what?
> {{RIVAL}}: Twelve weeks of walking west beside him. He wants rain on his city every year he lives —
> you hadn't heard?
> {{HERO}}: Rain every year? Who's he asking — the ancients? They've never answered one person in a
> thousand years!
> {{RIVAL}}: The mountain that's a door, and he has someone who can make them answer. You walk your one
> loaf — I ride, cart three!

**Verdict — a draw, leaning actors.** Splitting the lord's plan across two boasts instead of one speech
makes {{RIVAL}} sound like a man repeating what he has been told, which is what he is; "twelve weeks"
and "cart three" are the kind of number this character actually has.
The writer's exit is better: "Enjoy the walk!" beats the cut's cart number as a last line.

## 6. The Bread

**Writer**

> {{HERO}}: [pulling a folded cloth out from under the shelf] This is my father's cloth — that's his
> mark stitched in the corner!
> {{HEALER}}: He left it the year he died, then. Eleven years under a rock.
> {{HERO}}: He walked out here every year for thirty years and sat under this step. She knew him thirty
> years. Did he ever tell you that?
> {{HEALER}}: He never told anyone. And she has not taken the bread for a year, {{HERO}}. Whatever
> happened to her, she has been gone that long.
> {{HERO}}: He talked to her more than he talked to me.

**Actors (cut, from take 1)**

> {{HERO}}: [unfolds a cloth from under the shelf] That's my father's mark in the corner — he stitched
> it into everything he owned!
> {{HEALER}}: Last year's loaf is still wrapped, with a full year of rain on it. She has been gone a
> year, {{HERO}}.
> {{HERO}}: A year? Then he left this the year he died — it's been under this step eleven years.
> {{HEALER}}: Thirty years he sat under this step, and he told no one. She knew him longer than you did.
> {{HERO}}: Thirty years and he never once said her name to me? He talked to her every year and gave me
> the weather!

**Verdict — actors.** {{HEALER}} reasons from the object in front of her — wrapped loaf, a year of rain,
therefore a year gone — instead of announcing a conclusion, and "she knew him longer than you did" is
crueller and truer than "he never told anyone."
"He talked to her every year and gave me the weather" is the same feeling as the writer's last line with
a grievance attached, which is rule R5 working by itself.

## What the method does well

- **Private knowledge converts exposition into answers.** The best line the actors produced in six clips
  is "There is no carter." It exists because the clerk was told the board had to be cleared tonight and
  {{HERO}} was told to grab anything — so the carters' exodus arrived as a reply to a question instead
  of a clause at the end of a lecture.
- **Fact repair is reliable.** Three clips failed rule 0 on take 1. Each got one sentence of note added
  to the scene sheet's situation and each landed the missed fact on take 2, first try, in the right
  character's voice. That loop is cheaper than rewriting a clip.
- **Energy is free.** Every raw take came in over the 0c minimum without being asked twice. Actors given
  a want punctuate like people; a writer working alone drifts into declaratives.
- **Voices stay separate.** Across seven reads nobody wrote a line in the wrong character's rhythm.
  {{MENTOR}} never once asked a question.

## What it does badly

- **Actors spend what they were told to withhold.** {{MENTOR}} was told in writing that he was saving
  "you're not ready", and he said it anyway, because an actor with a want wants to win his last turn.
  A want will not hold a line back; only a prohibition will.
- **No one writes the joke.** Five of six takes contain no quip at all. A joke costs a turn and returns
  nothing to the speaker's want, so no actor volunteers one, and the cuts are drier than the writer's.
- **Everything is said twice.** Actors repeat the previous fact back before answering it. Raw takes run
  roughly 40% redundant, and all the compression work lands on the cut.
- **Bracket inflation.** Actors attach a stage direction to almost every line because it is free
  characterisation. The raw takes average six brackets per clip against a budget of one or two.
- **One reciter absorbs the scene.** In "The Board" the clerk carried five of nine facts because he is
  the character whose voice permits recitation. Facts drift to whoever is allowed to list things.

## How to improve the sheets

1. **Add a NEVER SAY block to each actor sheet.** Hart's should read: *in this scene you do not say the
   words "you're not ready", whatever he does.* Private motivation does not hold; an explicit ban does.
2. **Assign facts to actors, not to the scene.** Put "you are the one who says X" in the private sheet.
   The shared fact list should only be the checklist the director greps against, so that facts get said
   in the voice that suits them instead of by whoever recites first.
3. **Give one actor the joke, by name, every scene.** "You have the one quip in this clip; it goes
   before the vulnerable line or two lines after it." Otherwise there is no joke.
4. **Harden ACTOR_RULES.** Cap lines at eighteen words; allow a bracket only when the character changes
   the physical situation; forbid restating the previous line's fact as a question more than once per
   scene. That moves the compression out of the cut and into the take.
5. **Tell the last actor what the scene still owes.** The final turn is where facts get dropped — take 2
   of "The Board" lost "eleven years ago" on the very last clerk line. The closing prompt should name
   the unstated facts, the same way the take-2 note does.
6. **Two takes as standard, not as repair.** Take 2 was better than take 1 in all three clips that got
   one, and not only on the missed fact. Budget for it.
