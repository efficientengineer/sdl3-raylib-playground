# TREATMENT — the game, told the way you'd play it

Read this out loud if you can. It is the story as a player lives it, start to finish.

Names you have to remember: **Bron, Lyra, Zeph, Pip** — your party. And five others, total, in the
whole game. Everything else is called what it is: the company, the hunters' guild, the old halls
under the hills, the company city, the desert, the android, the man the company made.

---

## The story

**You're a young hunter.** Twenty-three, red hair, an axe on your back that's a size too big for
you, and a hall full of older hunters who think you took a stupid job. You did.

The game doesn't open with that. It opens with rain.

**Night, a bare hillside, a storm.** You're already running — the four of you, uphill, toward a
stone door set into the hill that is standing open and should not be. The contract says *clear the
entrance of the old halls, ask no questions, triple rate*, and the seal was burned off the bottom of
it with a lamp so nobody could read who wanted it done. You're forty feet from the door when
something comes out of it: low, fast, too many legs, pale as something that has never been outside.
You fight it in the rain in the first three minutes. Pip is already making jokes. Lyra is already
healing someone. Zeph is already delighted.

Then the lightning shows you the door properly, and the handprints in the dust on the floor go the
wrong way. It wasn't opened from outside. Somebody opened it from **in there**.

**You go down.** The floor of the entry hall turns out to be a lift the size of a courtyard, and when
it drops it takes the four of you a mile into the dark with an old maintenance arm swinging out of
the black at you on a schedule nobody ever cancelled. At the bottom it's lit. Swept. Warm. Somebody
has been mopping.

Deeper in, a dead man in ancient armour stands in a doorway and **doesn't attack you**. He lowers a
halberd across the gap and points back the way you came. Lyra works out why before you do: *it isn't
guarding the door from us; it's still guarding us from the door.* You go through anyway, because you
took the money. Past him, two more of his kind hold a corridor that isn't there anymore, and those
two you have to put down, and it feels bad.

Then you reach the last door, the one you were paid to open, and you brace for the monster.

**Behind it is a canvas tent.** A folding table, a lamp, a tin kettle, and a clerk with a clipboard
who looks up with mild annoyance and asks whether you have an entry chit. Behind him, a hundred
yards of company lamps run away down into the dark, and a survey crew is eating lunch. You were not
the heroes of this. You were the fuse. Somebody paid four hunters to trip the old defences so that
nobody on the payroll had to.

**Then the company is nice to you,** which is worse. The man who posted the contract is Cadder, a
field superintendent, a big warm funny man who sings when he walks. He buys you dinner, admits
everything cheerfully, pays in full plus damages for the door, and says: come up to the city and
sign for it properly, and bring your friends, and have a bath.

**The company city** is six tiers of warm windows stacked up a cliff face, lifts running all night,
a fair on the third tier, hot water in the walls. It is the nicest place you have ever been. And you
can lean on the tier rail and look straight down at the thing the city is built on: a boring head
the size of a cathedral, turning, chewing into the hill, hauling up what the company sells. Not ore.
**Records.** People, as patterns, lying in the rock where somebody put them a thousand years ago.
The company digs them out, files them, and sets them into paying customers.

You meet the chairman, an old man who is genuinely kind to a child at the fair and shakes your hand
with both of his. And you meet the woman who is your problem for the rest of the game: **Kerrow**,
junior director, white coat, unhurried, polite to the point of insult, who compliments your axe and
has already decided what to do about you.

Because Pip opens a door she shouldn't. Behind it is a ward: forty people in clean beds, fed, calm,
and not one of them can tell you their name. On the desk is a folder with the name of Bron's home
town on it, four inches thick.

**The escape** takes the rest of the evening: down through the machine floor with the company's
police coming the other way, out along a burning freight rail, and over the edge on a cargo line at
dawn with the lit city hanging above you like a chandelier. Kerrow watches you go. She doesn't
shout. She writes something down.

**Now the world opens.** A rail sledge out across a dry seabed on rails that run to the horizon, and
you ride it a full day with your legs over the side. Later a salt crawler on eight wheels, stolen.
Later a flier, also stolen. There are monsters out there, and dead machines standing up out of the
salt like teeth, and towns glad to see hunters.

And in every town the thing the company does shows through. A man on a bench talks to you pleasantly
for five minutes about his daughter and stops mid-sentence, because he has reached the part of the
story with her face in it and there's nothing there. He apologises. He's embarrassed. He asks if you
know her.

On the seabed you pick up an **android** — one of the old made people, sixty years older than the
company and very tired of it, eight small lights down her temple, five still lit. They go out across
the game, one by one, and none of them comes back on.

**And then, at a company bore out on the salt, the ground goes quiet.** A ring of stillness goes out
across the flats, and forty people standing in it stop being anybody. In the dust afterward you see
a man in a grey coat walking *toward* the bore while everyone else runs, and he is the most
frightening thing you have seen in this game so far, because nothing touches him. The company has a
standing notice out for him. Their file name for him is **the Fair Copy**. Do not converse.

**The erased town.** You finally get to the town Bron grew up in — Tellwater — and every face in the
square turns and smiles at you, and every one of them is one degree wrong. The baker grips his
shoulder with a floury hand and calls him by his mother's name too well. A woman calls him a
nickname he has never had in his life. Down in the drowned bore under the town there's a maker's
plate, and it's dated the year of the flood the flood was supposed to explain. Fourteen years ago
this town went blank in a single night — four hundred people in nine seconds — and the company
rebuilt it with hired employees on four-year postings and filed the night correctly. The baker is an
actor. He has been an actor for eleven years. He is also, by now, just a baker.

You run. The roads close. Cadder's cordon comes over the ridge, and Cadder is at the front of it,
and he is still funny, and he apologises while he does it, and that is the first time it really
hurts. He bought you dinner so he'd know where you'd go.

**The man the company made** catches up with you before you catch him. You watch him give a
fifty-eight-year-old tanner his whole life back in one piece, fourteen years of it at once, in the
street, in front of the man's wife — and it destroys him, and the man in the grey coat sits with him
for a day and a half afterward, which is longer than the company ever did. He isn't a monster. He's
a survey clerk who was at the bottom of the shaft the night the town went out; four hundred and ten
people went into him and he has carried them ever since. He apologises constantly. Under pressure
his voice arrives doubled.

You corner him on the salt flats and he doesn't fight. He sits down on a fallen ring with his hands
open and tells Bron about the winter Bron got lost in the snow when he was nine — in the first
person, with the timing right, with the one small detail Bron has never told anybody because it was
too small to tell.

**The reveal.** In the company's file room you find Bron's own intake form. *Age nine. Condition:
blank. Disposition: set and returned. Four marks.* The warm childhood you have been telling people
about for the whole game was issued to him out of a drawer to make a blank child presentable, and it
belongs to the man in the grey coat. And it's worse than that: Bron was standing over the open shaft
that night, not at the machine, so nothing of his was stored anywhere. Everyone else in that town
can, in principle, be given back. He can't. There is no copy. There never was.

His real mother presses laundry two streets away. He stands in her doorway and she holds out a
chipped cup of tea to a stranger and he doesn't take it and he doesn't tell her.

**Then the company closes the town.** Sixty postings end; sixty people pack up eleven years of a
life that was a job. There is no villain in the road that day — there's a nineteen-year-old with a
rifle and two days of training whose superintendent told him *nobody has to get hurt, just stand in
the road.* He fires once. **Lyra** dies in the dirt with her circlet knocked sideways, and she starts
the fifteen questions her order asks the dying, out of habit, on herself, and can't finish them. She
asks Bron to take her last hour, and he does, and it becomes the first memory in his head he is sure
is real, and it is not his. The boy who fired sits down beside his own rifle and can't get up. Pip
is holding her other hand.

**The crisis is the company trying to fix it.** Kerrow takes the chair, does the arithmetic nobody
else will do, evacuates four thousand people (saving them) and runs the big bore at full (which is
what breaks everything). Cadder disobeys and empties his own town and is relieved of his post in the
street, still trying to make the joke land.

**The descent** is a mile of stair, down past the doors that were sealed from the inside a thousand
years ago, into a city under the hills that is lit, swept, warm and fed — with three hundred and
forty healthy, calm people living in it who cannot tell you their names and have never held a memory
older than a week. They are the descendants of the people who built all this. The last council
didn't die: they wrote everything they were into the rock for safekeeping, sealed the doors behind
them, and waited to be read back out by somebody who never came. The singing that has drawn people
down here for a thousand years is mostly them, rehearsing.

At the bottom the man in the grey coat comes apart and speaks in four hundred frightened voices at
once, and the fight is not to beat him; it's to reach the person inside the noise. You do it by
asking Lyra's fifteen questions, in order, because that is what they always were — a way of checking
that a person moved intact — and also just somebody asking a frightened man who he is. Held
together, he chooses: he gives all four hundred records away, whole, once, into the seabed
chant-lines, sung, where nothing can be audited, seized, filed or burned. Zeph asks them to take it,
and sings for eleven hours, and cannot stand up afterward. Meanwhile Kerrow comes down the shaft to
cap the whole thing with everyone still inside, with the only costed plan anybody has produced, and
she is never actually refuted. She is stopped by **people**: Cadder on the stair with sixty of his
own crew, an old warden holding a door, a clerk somewhere filing a message log because it belongs in
the file.

**And nothing is undone.** No town comes back. Nobody's dead return. The company doesn't fall; it
has a bad year. The man in the grey coat is empty at last, and for the first time in fourteen years
he is not crowded, and he says his own name out loud — **Marek** — and you realise you have never
heard it, and neither had he. The android leaves to go bring one dead machine back up, which will
take her the rest of her life. The people from under the hills walk up a mile of stair by hand over
seven months.

The last thing you see is a new bill going up on the board in the guild hall, and Bron standing in
the doorway in a cold wind, keeping a borrowed childhood on purpose, out loud, because he's decided
it's his — he carried it.

## How this feels like PS4 / FF7

Five beats, matched to the *feeling* those games gave, not their plot.

1. **The tent behind the last door.** The feeling of the first hour of FF7: you think you're in a
   fantasy and you're actually in somebody's industry, and the reveal is funny before it's horrible.
2. **Riding the rail sledge out across a dry seabed with your legs over the side.** The PS4 feeling
   of a big sad beautiful world that used to work better than it does, and of four people who like
   each other crossing it.
3. **The android's lights going out, one a chapter, and never coming back on.** PS4's melancholy:
   old made things running down, doing their jobs anyway, without self-pity.
4. **Lyra in the road.** The feeling both games are famous for — not shock, but the long walk
   afterward with a hole in the party formation.
5. **A mile of stair down into a warm lit city that has been fine without us for a thousand years.**
   The late-game FF7 feeling of going somewhere the story has been pointing at since minute one, and
   of it being stranger and sadder than the villain was.

## What I dropped from the first draft and why

- **Roughly forty invented proper nouns.** Peoples, orders, guilds, regions, machines, company
  departments. They were the whole problem: a reader had to run a glossary to follow a scene. Five
  names survive, and four of them are people you either love or hate.
- **The reasonable-villains rule.** Kerrow is still correct, but she's now someone with presence you
  enjoy hating, and the chairman is charming, and Cadder is funny right up to the moment he betrays
  you. Reasons are not a substitute for menace and style.
- **"No theme lines."** The draft forbade anyone from saying what they felt, so nobody did, and it
  read as evasive. In this version people say what they mean, plainly, and the big lines are said
  out loud.
- **Four consecutive chapters that ended on a document.** The paperwork stays as texture — an intake
  form, a payroll page — but each act now turns on a picture: the cathedral machine, the wrong
  smiles, the ring of stillness on the salt, the man coming apart into voices.
- **The quiet opening.** The old first hour was a walk to a door. Now it's a storm, a monster, a
  mile-deep drop on a falling floor, and the joke of the tent — inside the first twenty minutes.
